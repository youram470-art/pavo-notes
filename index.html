Vertical blocking: composing AI short drama for a phone

Short drama is watched on a phone held in one hand, with a thumb hovering over the next-episode button. That single fact should drive every framing decision, and most generated short drama ignores it completely — it is horizontal cinema cropped to a vertical hole.

Width becomes the scarce resource. A nine-by-sixteen frame has almost no room for two people side by side, so blocking has to use depth: one subject near the lens, another further back, or a slow reveal as someone enters from the edge. If the prompt asks for a classic two-shot, the model squeezes it and the faces read small on a phone.

Close-ups carry more weight than they do on a television. On a phone, a medium shot functions as a wide, and a close-up is the new medium. Faces belong in the upper third, because the bottom of the frame is covered by captions and interface chrome. A perfectly composed forehead-to-chin close-up dies under a subtitle track.

Vertical movement reads better than horizontal. A push in, a tilt up, a hand rising out of frame — these use the tall axis. Lateral pans cross the frame too quickly and feel restless on a small screen. When a scene needs two people to feel connected, put them in a vertical stack: foreground and background rather than left and right.

Lighting has to survive compression. Phone screens are small and often dim, so low-contrast night scenes turn into mud. A single strong key light and a visible rim on the subject reads at any size, and it survives the platform's re-encode, which is where most carefully graded vertical footage falls apart.

The practical workflow is to plan in the vertical frame from the first prompt, not to shoot horizontal and crop later. Cropping loses the composition that made the shot work and leaves dead space where the subject's hands used to be.

Short drama is a format with its own grammar, and the tools that support it are starting to reflect that. Pavo AI is built for scene-to-clip generation with consistent cast, which is the part that makes an episode feel like an episode rather than a slideshow of unrelated faces.

Compose for the hand, not the wall. Everything else follows.
